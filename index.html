Ward air in motion

An animated version of a schematic figure showing how respiratory particles leave an infected patient, travel through the air of a hospital bay, and where ventilation, portable HEPA air cleaners, ultraviolet light and masks act. All the mitigations start switched off; switch each one on to see what it does.

What to look for

  • Particle size is continuous. The largest particles follow ballistic paths and land on the bed and bedside table within about a metre (direct deposition, then surface contact). Smaller particles stay airborne, densest near the source (short range) and carried through the shared air of the bay (long range).
  • Ventilation dilutes the air and the ceiling extract removes particles. The HEPA air cleaner draws air in low down and returns filtered air upwards.
  • Ultraviolet light inactivates particles rather than removing them: far-UVC (222 nm) can be used in the occupied zone, upper-room UV-C only above head height. Inactivated particles turn into open grey circles, with a brief violet flash at the moment of inactivation.
  • Source control: the masked patient on the right releases far fewer particles. Turn the mask on to compare.
  • Skin squames (grey flakes, 4 to 25 µm) are shed in very large numbers and resuspended by patient movement, and thrown higher into the air during a full linen change; many carry bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus. The hand wash sink releases splash and drain aerosol (blue).

Schematic, not a physical simulation: particle numbers, speeds, airflow and inactivation rates are illustrative and not to scale; it illustrates concepts and shows no measured or modelled results. Terminology follows the WHO 2024 global technical consultation on airborne transmission. Squame figures from Beggs and colleagues (2015). Colours are chosen to remain distinguishable with common forms of colour blindness. Accompanies a review on airborne infection on older people's wards, in preparation for Age and Ageing.
